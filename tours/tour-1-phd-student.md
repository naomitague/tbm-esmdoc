# Tour 1: PhD student exploring their region

**The question:** "Forests in my region are changing. What impact does that have on the water cycle, and what should I be reading?"

**Who:** An early-career researcher who knows their region but not yet the vegetation–water literature. Needs orientation more than answers.

## Step 1: Start on the home page 

**Page:** [[Home]]

**Do:** Click **Water Model**.

**Notice:**  We will come back to this but you can see how this works Framework model allows you to click on a topic and see if this included in specific models - more relevant 

> **Prototype note:** Later, a chat box could take a question right to the water cycle response to vegetation change page.

## Step 2: See the whole system

**Page:** [[Water cycle response to vegetation change]]

**Do:** Find this page under **Relationships**. Click the boxes on the concept map that match what's happening in your region.

**Notice:**  This gives current conceptual model and key considerations that will be important for understanding/predicting water cycle responses  to vegetation change. Click on **Evapotranspiration** to learn about basics of evapotranspiration (also in related content on right panel). Ultimately all of the boxes will have pages - Pages are general understanding of the flux/store/parameter - but are linked to ESMs and Observation pages

## Step 3: Zoom out to the global picture

**Page:** [[Global ET trends]]

**Do:** Look at the spread of published estimates of evapotranspiration trends. Filter by method and time period.

**Notice:** Globally, evapotranspiration is rising, partly because the planet is greening. But estimates disagree, and the disagreement depends on method. This is a key value of our system is that it does the synthesis of current studies - so we see the range of values (and new studies could be added)

## Step 4: Zoom in to watershed studies

**Page:** [[Watershed studies of disturbance effects on hydrology]]

**Do:** Filter to climates (so a PhD student in a given region can focus) and to the response you care about, such as evapotranspiration. Click a bar in the histogram to see the studies behind it.

**Notice:** See ranges of responses in certain environments - and/or few studies in particular climates/biomes - again value is all studies are in one place.

> **Prototype note:** Only 260 of the 484 observations have a climate class, so the climate filter shows about half the evidence.

## Step 5: Find out why responses differ

**Page:** [[What shapes responses]]

**Do:** Read through the 18 factors in six groups. Pick ones that apply in your region, so here click on snow - notice the topics (this is the kind of thing you'd see in a review paper = the different explanations for why behavior varies) and links to study - click on a study to see the citation

**Notice:** This summarizes that provide evidence that a 'caveat' e.g. post disturbance recovery rate matter - click on examples in controls and evidence

**Prototype note:** I just took a few studies for examples - this is where experts could start adding
## Step 6: Look at one process up close

**Page:** [[Evapotranspiration]]

**Do:** Read about the parts of the flux and the basic physics. Scroll to the table comparing how different land surface models calculate it.

**Notice:** The same process is represented quite differently from one model to the next.

> **Prototype note:** Some sections of this page are still being written.

## Step 7: See how it's measured

**Page:** [[Measuring evapotranspiration]]

**Do:** Read how evapotranspiration is measured and what the main limitations are.

**Notice:** Each measurement method sees the process at a different scale. If you like, return to the concept map and repeat steps 6–7 for another variable you care about.

> **Prototype note:** Some measurement pages are still partial.

## Step 8: Which models capture what matters? The vegetation side

**Page:** [[Vegetation model]]

**Do:** Choose a model from the menu. Check whether it represents the vegetation processes behind the factor you care most about from step 5.

**Notice:** Each box shades by whether the model represents that process: yes, partial, no, or needs verification.

## Step 9: The same model, the water side

**Page:** [[Water model]]

**Do:** Keep the same model selected and check the water processes linked to your factor.

**Notice:** To be useful for your question, a model has to get both halves right: the vegetation change and the water response.

## Step 10: The reverse direction

**Page:** [[Hydro to veg]]

**Do:** Return to the concept map and follow the arrow from water back to vegetation.

**Notice:** Water availability also shapes vegetation, for example through drought stress and mortality.

> **Prototype note:** This page is still an outline.

## What you leave with

A mental model of the system, a short list of the factors most likely to matter in your region, a sense of which observations and models could help, and a reading list built from the studies behind those factors.

## What's not here yet
