# Tour 2: Where does a new paper fit?

**The question:** "A new paper argues that with warming, more dieback will increase streamflow, especially in snow-dominated watersheds, because evapotranspiration declines. Is this new, and is it convincing?"

**Who:** A reviewer, or an author testing an idea before writing it up. Needs a fast survey of what's known, what methods to trust, and what to watch for.

## Step 1: Place the idea in the system

## Step 2: Check it against the global picture

**Page:** [Global ET trends](/wiki/evapotranspiration_patterns_global)

**Do:** Look at the published estimates of global evapotranspiration trends, then at the studies attributing those trends to greening on [Vegetation greening and global hydrology](/wiki/global_vegetation_change_et).

**Notice:** Globally, evapotranspiration is rising, partly because of greening. The paper claims the opposite locally. Is it describing a regional exception to a global pattern, and does it say why?

## Step 3: See what watershed studies have found

**Page:** [Watershed studies of disturbance effects on hydrology](/wiki/watershed_disturbance_synthesis)

**Do:** Look at all the observations first, then narrow to mortality or dieback, snow-dominated climates, and the region the paper covers. Click through to the studies.

**Notice:** How many studies already exist, and whether their results agree with the paper's.

> **Prototype note:** Only about half the observations can be filtered by climate. Check whether dieback can be separated from fire and harvest.

## Step 4: Ask whether the controls explain it

**Page:** [What shapes responses](/wiki/what_shapes_responses)

**Do:** Look at the factors the paper's argument depends on, especially snow regime, how fast vegetation regrows, and the forest's structure before dieback. Click the evidence counts.

**Notice:** Whether the existing evidence supports the conditions under which the paper's result should hold, and whether the paper considers the factors that could reverse it.

## Step 5: Does the model represent the vegetation change?

**Page:** [Vegetation model](/models/vegetation-som)

**Do:** Choose the model the paper used, or the closest one. Check how it represents mortality and dieback.

**Notice:** Whether dieback is simulated as a process or imposed from outside the model.

## Step 6: Does the same model represent the water response?

**Page:** [Water model](/models/water)

**Do:** Keep the same model selected. Check transpiration, evapotranspiration and snow under the canopy.

**Notice:** The paper's argument is a chain across both pages. If the model is missing any link, the conclusion rests on that gap.

## Step 7: Check the measurements

**Page:** [Measurements on the water model](/models/water)

**Do:** Find how the paper's key variables are measured and read the known issues for each.

**Notice:** Which measurement limitations could produce or hide the paper's result.

## Step 8: Check the evaluation

**Page:** [Spatial validation metrics](/wiki/spatial_validation_metrics)

**Do:** Read the strengths and weaknesses of the methods the paper uses to evaluate its products or models.

**Notice:** Whether the paper's evaluation is strong enough for its claims.

> **Prototype note:** The evaluation page is only a start.

## What you leave with

A quick survey of what has already been done, a list of related studies and methods papers to read, and specific questions to put to the authors.
