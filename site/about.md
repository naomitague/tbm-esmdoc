In environmental science, papers are themselves data: information we use in our many efforts to
understand what is and what might be. Papers, however, do not exist in isolation. They build on the
legacy of existing knowledge. Even those papers that might be seen as revolutionary rest on
conceptual and empirical foundations laid by earlier work — work documented mostly in peer review
literature.

Most papers are not revolutionary, but they are still valuable contributions to this knowledge
landscape. Some papers support or contradict working hypotheses, that is, our current conceptual
understanding of how environmental systems work. In environmental science, relationships often
change across time and space, and with the scales at which we examine them. As a result, we often
need to test how a hypothesis or idea holds up in multiple contexts. Papers that make measurements
and apply analyses in a specific setting, to test a hypothesis or quantify the size of an effect,
are therefore important contributions, and they are often lost in the sea of publications.

Other papers advance methods. Many environmental variables are hard to measure, such as root
networks and their access to water, so many papers add to the knowledge landscape by applying and
evaluating new methods and observational techniques. Many observations are indirect estimates,
especially in the modern era of widespread remote sensing products, comparing estimates made by
different techniques or evaluate approaches by reasoning about underlying assumptions. Still other
papers refine, shift, or adapt our conceptual models, showing how we can organize and simplify the
complexity of the environment. These papers act as lenses: they tell us what to focus on, what we
can ignore, and when.

A key challenge in the environmental science landscape is synthesis: how do we bring together what
we learn about different processes, and states/parameters in the environment and what controls how
they change? Integrated models have long served as one way to bring together multiple interacting
components of the environment. We often think of these integrated models (such as earth system
models) as prediction tools, but their real value may be as tools for synthesizing knowledge and
data, and for exploring the implications of different hypotheses, new findings, and new scenarios.

Conceptual models — and their representation as mathematical models — are the building blocks of
this knowledge landscape, and they come in two broad types. The first describes what controls a
specific process, such as evapotranspiration or precipitation, or a state or parameter, such as
biomass or soil water holding capacity. Many of these process-based models have a long history and
form a kind of textbook knowledge. Even so, the question of how simple or complex a conceptual or
quantitative model should be in a given context continues to open new areas of research. The second
type of conceptual model is organized around a research question, often framed as a relationship
that is scientifically or societally of interest. For example, how does vegetation change, whether
from fire, fuel treatment, drought mortality, or climate warming, influence water? How do plants
respond to drought? How does climate change impact snow and how does this change the timing and
magnitude of water availability? These are "big" questions that the environmental science community
works on collectively, and hundreds or even thousands of papers may address them.

To summarize, the environmental science knowledge landscape is made up of many interconnected
components. Heliopause provides a road map through it. Its design draws on the classic review and
perspective papers that have historically been how we summarize a topic. Unlike a review paper,
however, Heliopause keeps evolving and links multiple topics together. Its main components are
described below.

## Main components

| Component | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| Earth system model (ESM) landing page | Organizes Heliopause around the processes, states, and parameters found in at least some ESMs. It uses ESMs as a synthesis framework rather than describing any single model. |
| Community model pages | Link to specific ESMs and community models and show which processes, states, and parameters each one includes. |
| Process/state conceptual (and mathematical) models | For each process, state, or parameter, present current conceptual models from simple to complex, with key papers that give more in-depth support. |
| Observational methods | Explain how each process, state, or parameter can be measured at different scales, and the uncertainties, pitfalls, and errors involved. These may become tables, with links to sources for datasets and papers on evaluation. |
| Method evaluation | Summarize current thinking on how to evaluate estimation methods; also sections on model calibration, uncertainty analysis — citations on papers. |
| Question-driven conceptual models | Organize papers around relationships of scientific or societal interest, such as how vegetation change affects water. Papers are tagged by time–space scale and method, so users can find work relevant to their questions and early-career researchers can find gaps. |
| Synthesis and comparison across existing studies | Bring results from many studies together in comparable form, such as pooled effect sizes and cross-paper comparison tables, so users can see where findings agree, where they conflict, and how results vary with setting, scale, and method. Disagreements between sources are flagged rather than smoothed over. This also helps to identify settings (climate/biomes) that are underrepresented. |
| Guided tours | Walk users through the site from a particular starting point, for example a PhD student exploring how regional vegetation change affects water, a modeler, or a reviewer placing a new paper in the context of existing work. |
| Cross-links | Connect all of these pages into a network. For example, a user exploring how fuel treatments affect groundwater recharge can find relevant papers for their region, explore current conceptual models of what matters (such as the type of treatment), and see the strengths and weaknesses of the models and observations used to quantify these effects. |
